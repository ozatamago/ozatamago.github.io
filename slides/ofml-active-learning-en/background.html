Background — Introduction

Why we need a surrogate model that preserves protocol structure

We follow the paper's Introduction along the arc motivation → gap with existing methods → gap in observation structure versus the real world → proposal (OFML), interleaving figures and interactions. The body text reproduces the Introduction verbatim.

① Motivation

In closed-loop autonomous experimentation, the protocol itself changes mid-exploration

Motivation (Introduction, paragraph 1)

In recent years, the integration of AI and robotics has made closed-loop autonomous experimentation — running hypothesis generation, experiment execution, observation, and model update as a single cycle — increasingly practical[SDL][mobile][GPT5-lab]. In life-science and experimental-chemistry research, investigators do not explore a fixed experimental space just once; instead they advance their exploration while changing the experimental protocol itself — reagents, measurement conditions, and even the order of operations — in response to observed results[bofn]. Accordingly, a surrogate model for closed-loop autonomous experimentation must provide not only predictive accuracy within a single protocol but also data efficiency that reuses knowledge across different protocols and selects the next experiment from a small number of runs.

These two requirements — knowledge reuse across protocols and data efficiency — form the axis that runs through the surrogate-model design of this work.

② Gap with existing methods

There is room "in between" a full mechanistic simulator and a single black box

Gap with existing methods (Introduction, paragraph 2)

Existing approaches each face different limitations with respect to this requirement. A full physical/chemical simulator can treat the mechanism inside a protocol explicitly, but it is not easy to build for wet-lab experiments, which involve unknown parameters, unobserved intermediate states, and system-specific boundary conditions[E-CELL][whole-cell]. Black-box surrogate models based on Gaussian processes or neural networks, on the other hand, are convenient for optimization from small data, but when they treat a protocol as an identifier or a flat feature vector they lose structure such as operation order, operation sharing, and differences between measurement devices[reaction-BO]. Large language models, while useful for hypothesis generation and for exploiting literature knowledge[Coscientist][ChemCrow], do not by themselves naturally provide calibrated posterior uncertainty or an acquisition function aligned with protocol structure. For this reason, there remains room, between a full mechanistic model and a single black-box surrogate, for surrogate modeling that preserves protocol structure.

③ Gap in observation structure versus the real world

The surrogate model receives only the "terminal output" — it cannot tell which operation the error came from

Terminal measurement only: the states right after A / M are not recorded, so it is undetermined which operation the error of the terminal y should be attributed to. The researcher does in fact implicitly track the intermediate progress, but it leaves no trace as data and is not passed to the surrogate model.
Gap in observation structure (Introduction, paragraph 3)

This gap becomes even clearer under the observation structure characteristic of experimental science. In human-centered experiments, the experimenter implicitly manages the intermediate progress of operations through manual techniques, visual checks, environmental adjustments, and empirical judgments that are not written down in the protocol[tacit]. In closed-loop autonomous experimentation, however, the data the surrogate model receives is in many cases limited to the terminal output through a measurement device. When the intermediate states are not directly observed, the local model corresponding to each operation cannot be supervised on its own, and it is not obvious which operation the terminal error should be attributed to[DeepGP]. The more robotization increases the repeatability of experimental operations, the more this terminal-measurement constraint comes to the fore as the central problem in surrogate-model design governing operation-level knowledge transfer.

④ Proposal: Object-Flow Machine Learning

Treat a protocol as a structured input of "experimental objects, operation sequence, and measurement device"

Proposal (Introduction, paragraph 4)

In this work we propose Object-Flow Machine Learning (OFML) for this problem. Rather than treating an experimental protocol as a single identifier or a flat feature vector, OFML treats it as a structured input consisting of experimental objects, an operation sequence, and a measurement device. Concretely, when the same type of experimental operation appears in different protocols, OFML shares the parameters of the prediction map corresponding to that operation, and learns the prediction for the whole protocol against terminal measurements. Through this design, OFML realizes structured surrogate modeling that does not demand mechanistic knowledge as detailed as a full physical/chemical simulator, yet preserves the internal operation structure of a protocol better than a single black-box surrogate model.

Contributions of this paper (3 points)

1
Problem formulation

We formulate the protocol-structured surrogate-model learning problem for a set of multiple experimental protocols with terminal measurements.

Problem setting → 2
Requirements for knowledge transfer

We give the requirements under which knowledge transfer across protocols holds, from the perspectives of a shared operation vocabulary, operational semantics, local-input coverage, operation identifiability, and Lipschitz stability.

Theoretical statement → 4
Verification by controlled experiments

Using controlled experiments that separate the cases where the requirements hold or break, we verify the correspondence between the theoretical requirements and transfer performance, and evaluate how much the terminal-measurement-based active learning can correct the residual error in settings where the requirements are not fully satisfied.
